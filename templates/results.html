Evaluation

Results & Comparison

This evaluation uses the prepared ICU stay feature files for the academic prototype. The AI model now prefers patient_features_ai.csv when available, while the existing research evaluation artifacts remain available for comparison.

Because the system outputs Low/Medium/High risk while the dataset label is binary hospital mortality, we report two interpretations. High-risk detection treats only High as positive. At-risk detection treats both Medium and High as positive.

Baseline uses the SIRS (Systemic Inflammatory Response Syndrome) criteria (Bone et al., 1992). SIRS-positive is defined as 2 or more of 4 criteria (temperature > 38C or < 36C; heart rate > 90/min; respiratory rate > 20/min; white blood cell count > 12,000/mm3 or < 4,000/mm3), mapped here to Low (0-1 criteria), Medium (2 criteria, SIRS-positive), and High (3-4 criteria). Citation: Bone, R.C., Balk, R.A., Cerra, F.B., et al. (1992). "Definitions for sepsis and organ failure and guidelines for the use of innovative therapies in sepsis." Chest, 101(6), 1644-1655.

The baseline is the 4-criteria SIRS rule (Bone et al., 1992) described above. The modular agent-inspired workflow extends it with data validation, a separate weighted clinical scorer covering additional physiological/laboratory variables beyond SIRS, verification, explanation, and a workflow trace, using a fixed sequence of specialized rule-based components rather than autonomous multi-agent negotiation. The weighted scorer's factor weights reflect engineering judgment inspired by general ICU severity-scoring concepts (e.g., APACHE II, SOFA) and are not calibrated against this dataset or drawn from a specific published scoring table - a stated limitation, not a validated clinical score.

The AI model shows high recall but low precision, making the prototype more suitable for review prioritization and transparency than autonomous clinical prediction. The main workflow contribution is transparency, traceability, documentation, and auditability. The system does not provide diagnosis or treatment advice.

The modular workflow's improvement over the SIRS baseline is real but narrow: it affects only 1.5% of cases (15/1000), driven mainly by escalation logic that flags co-occurring critical vital-sign patterns in emergency admissions specifically - non-emergency cases show no change at all. With only 15 affected rows, the exact magnitude should not be treated as a stable, generalizable effect.
